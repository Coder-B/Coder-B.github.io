🌤️ A softer landing

The moment parents recognize

Why does my child get upset
when I pause a video?

You give a warning. You press pause. Then comes “one more!” Ending something enjoyable can be disappointing, and a young child may not understand how close the ending is. The next activity may feel unclear. A parent's decision to stop can also become another negotiation.

From the problem to the design

Make the ending easier to see.
Make the boundary consistent.

Two strands of research help explain this approach: how children learn the meaning of time, and how families experience the end of screen time.

01 · Understanding time

Knowing the word “minute” is different from knowing how long it lasts.

What researchers found. Tillman and Barner's 2015 experiments found that preschoolers could learn the ordering of time words while still having little understanding of the actual durations they represent. Saying “two more minutes” does not necessarily give a child a clear picture of the time remaining. Read the time-word study.

The numbers behind the time-word finding

Experiment 2 included 93 children ages 4–7, including 22 five-year-olds. Each child answered 30 comparison questions.

Five-year-olds: mean accuracy by comparison type
Comparison type (example)Correct answers
Same number: 2 minutes vs. 2 hours75%
Conflicting cues: 3 minutes vs. 2 hours61%

These are averages across multiple time-word pairs, not scores for the examples alone. The 14-percentage-point gap illustrates difficulty combining numbers with time units; it does not measure the benefit of visual timers. Author's dissertation reproducing the study, Chapter 1, Experiment 2.

What that suggests for design. Give the child a concrete cue to follow, alongside words and numbers. EarlyEdU's teaching guidance describes visible timers and first-then pictures as ways to prepare children for activity changes. This is practice guidance, rather than a trial of Gentle Bye. Read the visual-transition guidance.

In Gentle Bye

Kids Mode pairs the countdown with a train-and-track visual and shows the next activity. Parents can point to the shared cue while explaining that the viewing session is nearing its end.

02 · Accepting the ending

The stopping point can come from the session itself.

What researchers found. In a 2016 CHI family study of children ages 1–5, technology-triggered endings were associated with less child upset than parent-triggered endings. Examples included a DVD ending or a device battery running out. Read the screen-time transition study.

How children reacted when screen time ended

The 28 diary families recorded 380 screen-time transitions over two weeks. Parents rated reactions from 1 (very happy) to 5 (very upset).

Parent-reported reactions in the technology-versus-parent comparison
Ending triggered byDiary entriesMean rating
Technology432.98
Parent553.47

The difference was 0.49 points (reported p = .006); the association remained significant after accounting for session length (p = .011). Entry counts are events, not separate children. This is not a percentage reduction in tantrums or a test of Gentle Bye. Study results, PDF page 9.

What that suggests for design. Technology may help carry out a boundary established in advance, reducing the need for a parent to make a fresh stopping decision. The study was observational; it did not test automatic app timers against parents pressing pause.

In Gentle Bye

A parent chooses the total viewing limit before playback. When that limit is reached, the app pauses the video and starts the goodbye step. The design aims to reduce repeated negotiation while the parent helps the child move on.

Research informs the design. Children still respond differently.

The 2016 study also found that parent-delivered advance warnings were associated with more difficult transitions. It did not test whether visual reminders work better. The research cited here does not establish that Gentle Bye, its final 90-second reminders, or its goodbye ritual reduces tantrums. A child may still feel disappointed and need a parent's support.

Putting the ideas into practice

A visible journey.

Good to know

Questions parents ask

Research, everyday screen-time questions, and how Gentle Bye works.

Does my toddler understand “two more minutes”?

Not necessarily. Research on time words shows that preschoolers may know that an hour is longer than a minute while still struggling to connect those words to actual durations. A visible cue gives parents something concrete to point to when explaining the approaching end. See the research and design explanation.

Can a visual timer help my child stop watching videos?

A visual timer can make remaining time more concrete and is used in early-childhood transition guidance. Understanding the cue does not guarantee a child will be happy to stop. Gentle Bye combines a countdown and train-and-track visual with a next-activity prompt; its effect on screen-time tantrums has not been established by the research cited here.

Is an automatic ending easier than a parent pressing pause?

In a small observational family study, parent-reported reactions averaged 2.98 for technology-triggered endings (43 diary entries) versus 3.47 for parent-triggered endings (55 entries), on a scale from 1 (very happy) to 5 (very upset). This association does not prove automatic timers cause smoother transitions. Gentle Bye pauses playback at the limit a parent sets in advance.

Where can I download Gentle Bye?

Gentle Bye: Kids Timer is available for iPhone and iPad on the Apple App Store. Check the listing for current pricing, compatibility, and availability in your region.

How does Gentle Bye help end screen time?

A parent selects the videos, sets one total viewing limit, and chooses the next activity. Gentle reminders begin during the final 90 seconds. When time runs out, playback pauses and the child is guided through a goodbye step.

Does Gentle Bye limit other apps or all device screen time?

Gentle Bye times the video session inside Gentle Bye. It does not set a device-wide screen time limit or block other apps.

What is Gentle Bye?

Gentle Bye is an iPhone and iPad app that helps a parent choose public YouTube videos, set one total viewing limit, and prepare a child for a calm transition when the session ends.

Does it require an account?

No. Gentle Bye does not require a name, email address, or password. The app uses an anonymous installation identifier to operate sessions.

Does it decide what my child watches?

No. A parent searches for, reviews, and selects every video. Search uses YouTube's strict SafeSearch setting, but parents remain responsible for choosing appropriate content.

How do parents leave Kids Mode?

Parent controls use Face ID or Touch ID. If biometric authentication is unavailable or canceled, Gentle Bye provides a simple addition challenge for the parent.
